The audit returns attributes in priority order. The order is the product, and it is not the order
you would get by counting blanks.

## Why not just fix the emptiest column?

Because emptiness and usefulness are different quantities, and on a real shelf they invert.

Here are two attributes from the 216-product laptop catalogue we use for our own research:

| Attribute | Missing on | Rank by emptiness | Rank by decision value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Processor | 162 of 216 | **1st** | **last** |
| Screen size | 80 of 216 | 4th | **1st** |

Processor is missing twice as often and almost nobody is deciding on it: it appears in 2 of the 32
buying requirements we tested. Screen size appears in 13. Fill processor everywhere and you have
done the most visible work on the dashboard and moved the fewest decisions.

<Callout type="warn" title="This is the whole argument">
  A completeness score measures your catalogue against itself. The audit measures it against what
  shoppers are trying to decide. Those produce different work orders, and only one of them is
  ranked by what it buys.
</Callout>

## What is a bit of decision information?

One bit is one halving of the shelf.

If a shopper faces 216 products and an attribute splits them cleanly in two, that attribute is
worth one bit: it takes 216 to 108. Two bits takes it to 54. The audit reports what each attribute
would be worth **if it were recorded everywhere**, minus what it is worth today, and the
difference is what the repair buys.

This is why the number is not a percentage. A percentage tells you how full a column is. Bits tell
you how much closer a shopper gets to a decision when you fill it.

## Where does the missing value already live?

Often in text you already own. Before recommending that anyone type anything, the audit tries to
recover the value from your own titles and descriptions.

On that same laptop shelf, **161 of 216 missing screen sizes were already sitting in the product
titles**. That is a parse, not a procurement project, and it changes what the repair costs enough
to change whether it is worth doing.

The audit reports each attribute's provenance so you can tell the two apart:

| Where the fact is | What the repair actually is |
| --- | --- |
| Already in your text | Extraction. Cheap, and often same-day |
| In a supplier feed | Mapping. A ticket, not a project |
| Nowhere | Acquisition. Someone has to go and find it |

## When is the ranking wrong?

Three cases, and we would rather you heard them from us.

<Steps>

<Step>

### The recorded values are not representative

Simulating a complete catalogue can only draw on values your shelf already holds. If an attribute
is recorded on twenty products and those twenty are not typical of the range, the simulation is
wrong in a way we can name and cannot yet correct.

</Step>

<Step>

### The requirements are not yours

The buying requirements we test against are typed from public category guides and filter rails. If
your shoppers want something those do not cover, the ranking will not know. Sending us your own
search and filter logs is what fixes this, and it is the single highest-value thing you can add.

</Step>

<Step>

### The fact exists only in a photograph

A dietary claim printed on a pack, a material visible in an image. We can tell you the attribute
is missing from your fields. We cannot yet read it out of your imagery, and we will say so on the
row rather than quietly scoring it as absent.

</Step>

</Steps>
